The gig is Friday and you don't have a solo. What now?

Updated 1 November 2026

Don't plan to improvise it. Settle on one solo in the song's key and tempo tonight, learn it by ear over the next three nights, and play that same solo on Friday.

Four nights is enough to get a solo of 8 or 16 bars under your fingers, as long as you stop hunting for the perfect one early. A solo you know inside out beats a better one you half know. The plan below assumes you can play, and that the problem is having nothing to play.

Tonight: map the gap, then settle on a solo

  1. Get the facts. The song's key, its tempo, how many bars the solo gets, the chord it starts on and the cue that ends it. Ask the band if you're not sure. Every later decision depends on these.
  2. Pick where the solo comes from. There are three honest places to get one. Choose one now.
    • The record. If it's a cover and the original has a solo the crowd expects, learn that one, or its first and last phrases with your own middle. A slow-down app on the recording does the heavy lifting.
    • The melody. Sing a line over the chords, then find it on the neck. Take a phrase of the vocal melody and vary it. This works best when you can already hear what you want and only need the time to find it.
    • A generated solo. When there's no solo to copy and nothing comes when you sing, start from a generated solo in the song's key and tempo and learn that. This is where FretShed fits. More on it below.
  3. Settle on one solo before you go to bed. Not the best solo you can imagine. One you like enough to play four nights running.

Whichever route you take, the next three nights are the same: learn it by ear, fit it to the song, and lock it in.

Night two: learn it by ear, one phrase at a time

  1. Listen through twice before you pick up the guitar. Hear where the solo breathes. Those gaps are your phrase boundaries.
  2. Loop the first phrase. Find its first note by ear, then the shape of the line.
  3. Slow it down. Drop the speed until you can play the phrase cleanly, then push it back up toward tempo.
  4. Join the phrases. When two phrases are under your fingers, loop them together. Keep adding the next phrase until the whole solo runs without a stop.
  5. Hum it away from the guitar. In the car, in the shower. If you can sing it, you can find it again on stage when your hands get nervous.

No tabs. Writing it down feels like progress, but on Friday you won't be reading. A solo you work out by ear stays in your hands.

Night three: make it fit the song

  1. Play it over the song itself, the recording or a rehearsal take, not only a backing track.
  2. Fix the clashes. Any note that fights a chord, move it a step or hold the one before it longer.
  3. Fit the length. Trim a phrase or repeat one until the solo fills the bars exactly.
  4. Land the ending. The last note should hand back to the band on the cue: a held note, a bend to the root, a phrase that leads into the next section.
  5. Make it yours. Change the feel of a phrase, add a bend you always play, move the climax later. These changes stick because you made them.

Night four: lock it in

  1. Play the solo in the song, from the top of the song. Coming in cold from the verse is what Friday will feel like.
  2. Stand up, with the guitar and gear you're taking. Your hands sit differently with the guitar on a strap, and bends and stretches feel different. Home is rarely gig volume, so get one loud run in at rehearsal or soundcheck if you can.
  3. Record a run and listen back. You'll hear the rushed phrase you can't feel while playing.
  4. Decide your bail-out. If you lose your place, where do you land? Pick a note you can hold until the next phrase starts, so a slip costs you a bar, not the solo.
  5. Stop changing it. From tonight, the solo is the solo.

Friday

Warm up with the opening phrase. Play the solo you learned. Don't rewrite it in the van.

What to know before you rely on it for a gig:

  • It matches genre, key and tempo, not chord changes. It won't follow a particular song's progression, which is why night three exists.
  • Tempo comes in steps of 10, from 60 to 120 bpm. Pick the nearest. Once the solo is under your fingers, you play it at the song's tempo anyway.
  • A solo is 30 seconds. At 120 bpm in 4/4 that's 15 bars; at 90 bpm, about 11. Trim or repeat a phrase to fit the section.
