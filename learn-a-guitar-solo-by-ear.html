How to learn a guitar solo by ear

Updated 15 November 2026

Work it one phrase at a time. Loop a phrase, slow it down without changing the pitch, sing it, find the first note and the shape of the line, and bring it back up to tempo before you move on to the next.

This page is for players who can already get around the neck. Your hands aren't the problem. The hard part is hearing a line clearly enough to find it, and that's the part that gets better with every solo you work out.

Before you pick up the guitar

  1. Listen through a few times. Don't try to learn anything yet. Get the whole solo in your head, start to finish.
  2. Mark where it breathes. Every solo has gaps where the player stops or holds a note. Those are your phrase boundaries, and each phrase is one job.
  3. Find home. Listen for the note the solo keeps landing on. Play single notes along with the track until one sounds settled. That's usually the key, and it tells you where on the neck to start looking.

Work one phrase at a time

  1. Loop the first phrase only. A bar or two, from one breath to the next. Let it repeat until you stop noticing the loop point.
  2. Slow it down. Drop the speed until you can hear every note. The pitch stays where it is, so what you find at half speed is what you play at full speed.
  3. Sing it. Hum the phrase along with the loop before you touch the guitar. If you can't sing it, you can't hear it yet. Slow it down further.
  4. Find the first note. Hunt for it on one string, comparing each note with the recording until they match. Everything else hangs off that note.
  5. Follow the shape. Does the line go up or down? Does it move by step or jump? Try the position you'd play in for that key first, and only move when the notes stop fitting.
  6. Get the rhythm, not only the notes. Where does the phrase start against the beat? Which notes are held, which are short? A phrase with the right notes and the wrong rhythm is a different phrase.
  7. Get the feel. Listen for how each note starts and moves. Is it picked or slurred? Does it slide in? Is that a bend or a fretted note? For a bend, fret the note you think it reaches and compare.
  8. Bring it up to tempo. Raise the speed in small steps. Move on only when the phrase is clean at full speed.

Loop it. Slow it down. Play along. Then do it again with the next phrase.

Join the phrases

  1. Loop two learned phrases together. The join is where solos fall apart, because you've only ever started each phrase fresh.
  2. Add the next phrase. Keep extending the loop until the whole solo runs without a stop.
  3. Start from different places. Play from the third phrase, then the second. If you can only play it from the top, you don't have it yet.

Make it stick

  • Hum it away from the guitar. In the car, in the shower. If you can sing it, you can find it again on the neck.
  • Play it the next day before you listen to it. Whatever you've lost is what to loop again.
  • Record a run and compare it with the original. You'll hear the rushed note and the bend that falls short, which you can't hear while you're playing.
  • Don't write it down. No tabs. Writing it down feels like progress, but if you can see it, you're reading it, not hearing it. Work it out by ear and it stays in your hands.

When a phrase won't come

  • A fast run you still can't hear. Slow it to half speed and find its first note, its last note and roughly where it sits on the neck. Fill in from both ends. A run that starts and lands right, with your own notes in the middle, is a fair version while your ear catches up.
  • The note is right but sounds wrong. Check the octave and the string. The same pitch on a different string has a different tone, and it can put the next note out of reach.
  • You've been on one phrase for twenty minutes. Leave it. Learn the next phrase and come back. It's often clearer once you know what comes after it.

What to learn by ear

Any solo works, but the material changes what you get out of it.

  • Solos you already know. Good for getting a favourite solo under your fingers. Your memory fills the gaps, though, so your ear does less of the work. For a solo on a record, a slow-down app that loads your own audio is the right tool.
  • Solos you've never heard. Nothing to fill in from memory, so every note is your ear's job. Your ear needs unfamiliar material to grow. Familiar material only confirms what you already know.
